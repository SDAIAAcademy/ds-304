How the work is scored

The rubric

Framing and floor
15%
Methods
25%
Backtest design
25%
Uncertainty and communication
25%
Reproducibility
10%
Every claim is read against the floor. A score without the floor beside it, or an interval without its measured coverage, earns nothing.

Full marks, criterion by criterion. Framing and floor: the decision, the horizon and the cost of each kind of error are stated, and the floor is the strongest naive method for the data. Methods: three families that differ in what they can see. Backtest design: rolling origins, an argument or a test that nothing after the origin is used, no selection on the test folds. Uncertainty and communication: coverage in counts, a note with limits. Reproducibility: runs from a clean copy, numbers traceable to files, licences stated.

Format of the defence

Eight minutes to present, four minutes of questions

1 · The decision

Who reads the forecast, what they decide, the horizon, the floor.

2 · What you built

The backtest and three method families: what each can see.

3 · What you measured

The scorecard: point and probabilistic accuracy against the floor.

4 · The note

The fan chart, coverage in counts, when not to use it.

Peer feedback. After each defence, everyone writes one strength, one question and one suggestion.

Two minutes for each part is a fair guide. Stop a presenter who spends four minutes on model internals: the rubric gives methods a quarter of the marks and the planner none of their attention.

The four minutes of questions

The assessor moves the origin or the horizon: predict, then show

The origin moves

"Forecast from the day before the last holiday. What happens to the error and to the note?"

The horizon moves

"The planner needs twice the horizon. What changes in the features, the floor and the bands?"

The history shrinks

"This series is six weeks old. Which of your families still works?"

First say what you expect and why. Then run it. A prediction that turns out wrong and is explained scores above a run without a prediction.

This is the authenticity check of the course: the participant predicts the effect, then shows it from their own repository. Ask one question per participant and let them run the backtest live; a backtest written as a function of the origin and the horizon answers in under a minute. If the run cannot be made, ask for the reasoning and note it under reproducibility.

Strongest patterns

Common gaps

What most would fix next. Usual ones: coverage never measured, a choice made on the test folds.

One surprise

After the course

Keep going

  • The follow-up task: backtest one forecast from your workplace, or a public one, against a seasonal-naive floor, and send the table.
  • Your repository stays yours: keep it running from a clean copy and add the next family you named today.
  • At work: archive what was known on each origin day, starting now. An honest backtest next year depends on it.
  • Read on: Forecasting: Principles and Practice, chapters 11 and 13, for weighted reconciliation and practical issues.

The follow-up task is tracked: say how and by when the table is sent, as your delivery team has set it. If the forecast is from the workplace, remind participants that the data stay inside their entity: the table of scores is what is shared, never the series.
