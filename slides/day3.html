The frame for today. Eight weeks held out: two folds of 28 days, with origins on 5 November and 3 December 2025. Every method is scored on the same feeders and the same days.

Three kinds of feeder: residential, commercial, industrial. A region's demand is the sum of its feeders. Say the limit of the evidence now: two folds, both in the mild season. The scores of today are not comparable with the 13-fold year of the regional labs, and a difference of a few hundredths between two methods is inside the spread between the folds.

Why two levels

Purchasing reads the region; operations read the feeders

Omar Al-Khuzaim · energy purchasing

Nominates energy by region. He needs the regional total and its upper quantile.

Hamad Al-Dawsari · network operations

Plans maintenance and switching by feeder. He needs each feeder's next four weeks.

The problem. If the feeder forecasts of a region do not add up to the regional forecast, two departments plan on two different futures.

Forecasts that add up across a hierarchy are called coherent. The data are coherent by construction: the meters add up. Forecasts are not, unless something makes them so. Ask the room which of the two numbers they would trust if they disagreed; do not settle it yet.

These are called base forecasts. The feeder models see a weekly pattern only; the regional model sees temperature and the calendar. A gap of 3% of a region's demand is larger than the margin purchasing works with. U.sf_many fits one statsforecast model to every column of a wide table.

Reconciliation

Two simple ways to make them add up

Bottom-up

Keep the feeder forecasts. The region is their sum.

Throws away the regional model, with its temperature and calendar.

Top-down

Keep the regional forecast. Split it by each feeder's share of the feeder forecasts, day by day.

Throws away the level of every feeder model, and keeps its share.

share = feeders_fc.div(feeders_fc.sum(axis=1), axis=0)
top_down = share.mul(region_fc, axis=0)

Textbook top-down uses historical shares. The lab uses the shares of the forecasts themselves, which keeps each feeder's weekday pattern: an industrial feeder that rests on Friday still rests on Friday. Each way keeps one level and discards the other: ask what a method that keeps both would look like.

Use both levels

Least squares: move every forecast as little as possible

  • Find the coherent set of forecasts closest to the base forecasts, in squared distance.
  • For one region with n feeders the answer is closed: every feeder takes an equal part of the gap.
  • With 37 feeders the total lands almost on the regional forecast.
n = feeders_fc.shape[1]
gap = region_fc - feeders_fc.sum(axis=1)
least_sq = feeders_fc.add(gap / (n + 1), axis=0)
The equal share is the weakness. A feeder of 30 MWh and one of 175 MWh receive the same correction in MWh.

This is ordinary least squares reconciliation. Weighted variants (by the variance of each series' errors, known as MinT) correct the equal share; they are in Forecasting: Principles and Practice, chapter 11, and in the hierarchicalforecast package. The course stops at the closed form because it can be written in three lines and checked by hand.

Sources: Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice, 3rd edition, chapter 11; Wickramasuriya, Athanasopoulos and Hyndman (2019).

02
One model for many series

A local model learns one series; a global model learns from all of them

Local: one model per feeder

388 fits. Each knows three years of one feeder. It cannot learn what Eid does from the feeder next door.

Global: one model, all feeders

One fit on every feeder's rows. An Eid seen on 388 feeders over eight years is thousands of examples.

The price. A global model needs a table: one row per forecast to make, with columns that describe it. Building that table is where the work and the risk are.

The winning entries of the M5 competition (30,490 retail series) were global boosted-tree models; that result is why this family is in the entry. The statistical models of Day 2 are local. Ask: what would a new feeder with eight weeks of history get from each kind? That is Lab 4.2.

Source: Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos (2022), the M5 accuracy competition.

Why the target is scaled

Divide each feeder by its own recent level, and the series look alike

Three feeders of Al-Dana, MWh a day: means of 33, 78 and 175.
The same three, each divided by its mean.

The model predicts demand divided by the mean of the 28 days before the origin, and the forecast is multiplied back. Lagged features are scaled the same way. The stretch task trains on raw MWh: the score goes from 0.62 to 3.75, because no feature says how large a feeder is and the trees spend their splits on size.

Pretrained zero-shot forecasters

  • Run a pretrained forecaster zero-shot on held-out weeks
  • Record accuracy, quantile scores and run time

03
A third family

A forecaster trained on other people's series, used on yours as it is

Pretraininga large collection of real and synthetic series, done once by the publisher
→
Contextyou pass the recent history of one series
→
Forecastquantiles for the next days. No training on your data
Zero-shot means the model has never seen this series or any series like it from you. What it knows of your data is the context you pass in.

These models are often called time series foundation models. Families named in the entry: Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai. They differ in architecture and in what they accept; the lab uses the smallest member of one family because it runs on a processor. Treat the model as one more baseline: it enters the same backtest and gets the same columns as every other method.

Sources: Ansari et al. (2024), Chronos: Learning the Language of Time Series; Das et al. (2024), A decoder-only foundation model for time-series forecasting; Woo et al. (2024), Unified Training of Universal Time Series Forecasting Transformers.

Read the interface before the scores

The model in the lab sees one thing: the series' own past

It can use

Level, trend and repeating patterns inside the context: the week, and the year when the context is long enough to hold one.

It cannot know

That Eid falls in the horizon. That a heat wave is expected. What kind of feeder this is. No calendar and no weather are passed in.

Predict before you run. Where should a model like this do well against the floor, and where should it lose?

Take predictions from the room and write them down; the lab's by-fold scores test them. Newer members of these families accept covariates (Chronos-2 does); the small model of the lab does not. The context in the lab is the last 512 days, which holds one full year.

Running it for a year

A model ages from the day it is trained: measure what each week of age costs

Replay a year48 weekly origins of 2025; a model trained at every one
→
Apply a scheduleeach origin is forecast by the newest model the schedule allows
→
Pair by originits error less that of a model trained the same week
Why pair. Old models forecast late in the year, new ones early. Comparing on the same origins removes the season from the gap.

The regional backtests of Days 1 and 2 refit at every origin; the boosted model of Lab 3.2 was trained once and served both folds. In operation the desk forecasts every week and must decide how often to train again: every run, every month, every quarter, or once. More training costs run time and a job to keep working; less training risks a model that has gone stale. The script labs/_tools/retrain_schedules.py replays this for the boosted model of Lab 3.2, with the same recipe, on all 388 feeders. The rule. Measure the gap by age before choosing a schedule, then keep watching it: retrain early when the live error leaves the band of the backtest.

Left: the model of the last slide. Every feature, and the level the forecast is multiplied by, is recomputed from the 28 days before each origin, so age costs nothing measurable. Right: the same forecasts, multiplied by each feeder's level on the training day instead of on the origin. That is what any model does that fixes its level when it is fitted and is not given the recent level as an input. Most of this gap is the season: a level read in winter is applied to summer. Within a month the gap is 0.175; from 4 weeks on it is larger than the fresh model's whole error of 0.801. Kept that way, retraining every 4 weeks would cost 0.136 against weekly, and once a year 1.972. How often to retrain depends on the model's inputs, not on its family. The gap shrinks after 26 weeks only because the season turns back towards the level the model was trained on.

Which choice can be defended?

Skill against the floor on 200 series: X 0.21, Y 0.20, Z 0.05. X is a pretrained model under a non-commercial licence. Y is a boosted model under a permissive licence. Z is the fastest. The forecasts will run a utility's weekly purchasing. Which family?

X: it has the highest skill
Z: it is the fastest of the three
Y: within 0.01 of the best, and its licence permits the use
X now, and ask about the licence after the pilot
Why. A licence is a gate, not a weight: a method that may not be used has no score. Among the methods that pass, 0.01 of skill is inside any noise.

Answer: C. The licence was read first on purpose in Lab 3.3. Ask what else is a gate and not a weight in the participants' own organisations: data residency, an approved-software list, a model that must be explainable to a regulator.
