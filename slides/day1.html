How this course works

Uncertainty is part of the forecast

Session 1

What is forecast, and why

  • Separate a forecast from a plan and a target
  • Fix horizon, granularity and level from the decision
  • Turn the cost of over- and under-forecasting into a target quantile
01
Before any model

A forecast, a plan and a target are three different numbers

Forecast

What you expect to happen, with its uncertainty. It can be wrong; it must not be biased on purpose.

Plan

What you decide to do about it: how much energy to buy, which day to maintain a feeder.

Target

What someone wants to happen. A target that leaks into a forecast makes both useless.

What goes wrong. "Give me one number" hides which of the three is asked for. Ask what will be done with it.

Ask the room for a case where a forecast was adjusted until it matched the budget. Most rooms have one. The point for the week: the desk delivers the forecast and its uncertainty; the planner turns it into a plan. Lab 1.1 practises asking "what will you do with it?" on three real-sounding requests.

Horizon is how far the furthest forecast is; the time step is the granularity; the level is where in the hierarchy. The fourth column is the one people skip: a total, an upper bound and a peak are different statistical objects. A peak over 90 days is an extreme, driven by growth and by how hot the summer is.

Which number to hand over

Unequal costs move the answer away from the middle

def target_quantile(cost_under, cost_over):
    return cost_under / (cost_under + cost_over)

target_quantile(2.0, 0.5)   # Omar: 0.8

A missing MWh costs Omar two contract prices more; a surplus MWh loses half a price.

This is the newsvendor result: the forecast that minimises expected cost is the quantile cu / (cu + co). Do the arithmetic on the board for Omar: 2 / 2.5 = 0.8. Consequence for the week: a point forecast is not enough, and the measure that scores a quantile (pinball loss, Day 2) is the one the purchasing lead cares about. None of the three planners should be handed the mean.

Responsible use

The finer the level, the closer the series is to a household

  • A regional total describes a city. A meter describes when a family is home.
  • A feeder that serves a handful of customers sits between the two.
  • Before a fine-grained series leaves the desk, ask the data protection officer whether it can be linked to a person, and so falls under the Personal Data Protection Law.

In this course

Every series is synthetic and aggregated: regions and feeders, never meters. The capstone uses supplied data, or public data under its stated open-data terms.

Keep this short and concrete: the question to ask is "can this series, alone or joined with another table, point to a person or a household?". The course does not teach the law; it teaches the habit of asking Thamer, the data protection officer, before using or sharing fine-grained consumption. Why does last year's pattern miss Eid?

A model with a 365-day season forecasts Al-Rimal for spring 2026. It is far too high on a few days in March and too low on a few in early April. What is the likely cause?

On the log scale. Summer swings are larger in MWh when the level is higher, so the parts multiply. Taking the logarithm turns the product into this sum.

Additive or multiplicative is the first choice in any decomposition. The test is a plot: does the seasonal swing grow with the level? In Al-Rimal it does. On the log scale a remainder of -0.10 reads as "about 10% below what trend and seasons say". Ask where a holiday that moves would land: it has no part of its own, so it lands in the remainder.

The tool

A decomposition with two seasonal periods, robust to odd days

from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import MSTL

res = MSTL(np.log(y), periods=(7, 365),
           stl_kwargs={"robust": True}).fit()

res.trend
res.seasonal["seasonal_7"]
res.seasonal["seasonal_365"]
res.resid
  • Each season is smoothed over its own cycles and may change slowly over the years.
  • Robust fitting gives an odd day little weight, so an outage stays in the remainder and does not bend the season.
  • The four parts add up to the log of demand exactly. Check it.

MSTL repeats a seasonal-trend decomposition by local regression once per period. The lab passes two more settings that limit the number of robust iterations, to keep nine years under ten seconds on a laptop; the slide leaves them out. Without "robust" the outage of March 2023 leaks into the annual season and reappears, faintly, every March.

Sources: statsmodels, MSTL; Forecasting: Principles and Practice, 3rd edition, chapter 3.

A number per part

Strength compares a component with the noise around it

def strength(component, remainder):
    ratio = remainder.var() / (
        component + remainder).var()
    return max(0.0, 1 - ratio)

Structural breaks

Al-Mina grew 52% in two years: a step, not a trend

  • On 1 July 2022 six industrial feeders joined the region.
  • A decomposition smooths the step into a steep trend.
  • A model trained across the step reads it as fast growth, or averages two levels.

Options

Train after the break. Or tell the model: a step regressor, or forecast the new feeders apart.

How you find out

The yearly means, then a question to network operations.

Lab 1.3 step 5: growth of the yearly mean from 2021 to 2023 is 51.8% in Al-Mina; the next region is at 10%. The labs train on a window of three years (1,096 days) for this reason among others: by 2025 the window lies after the break. In the capstone, participants are expected to look for breaks and say what they did about them.

04
The benchmark floor

Before any model, find out how good "no model" is

It costs nothing

No fitting, no tuning, no upkeep. It cannot break in production.

It is often good

On strongly seasonal series, repeating the right past period is hard to beat.

It sets the price

Every later method reports its distance to the floor, not its own error alone.

What goes wrong. "Our model has a 4% error" says nothing until you know that repeating last year gives 5%, or 3%.

This is the habit the course is named for. Participants met a baseline in DS-204; the forecasting version has a twist: there are several naive forecasts, and which one is the floor depends on the series and the horizon. That is what Lab 1.4 finds out.

Three ways to repeat the past

Naive repeats the last day; seasonal naive repeats the last cycle

def naive(train, h):
    return np.repeat(train.iloc[-1], h)

def seasonal_naive(train, h, m):
    last = train.to_numpy()[-m:]
    return np.resize(last, h)
MethodNext Friday equals
Naivethe last day seen
Seasonal naive, m = 7last Friday
Seasonal naive, m = 364the Friday 52 weeks ago
364, not 365. Fifty-two whole weeks keep a Friday on a Friday.

np.resize repeats the last m values when the horizon is longer than the cycle: a 28-day forecast with m = 7 is last week four times. Ask which of the three the decomposition table predicts will win in Al-Rimal at 28 days, and why.

The instrument

Move the origin through the last year and forecast 28 days each time

One fold. Solid: what happened. Dashed coral: last week repeated. Teal: the same weekdays a year earlier.
  • The origin is the last day the forecaster has seen.
  • 13 origins, 28 days apart: every day of 2025 is tested exactly once.
  • Each fold trains on the 1,096 days before its origin.
  • Nothing after the origin may reach the forecast.

The fold shown starts on 18 June 2025: demand climbs into summer, last week's values stay flat and fall behind, last year's values climb with it. One fold is an anecdote; thirteen are a measurement. This is time series cross-validation: unlike the shuffled folds of DS-204, the order of time is kept and training always ends before testing starts. The last bullet is the rule Day 4 audits.

Sources: Forecasting: Principles and Practice, 3rd edition, time series cross-validation.

The measure

A scaled error lets a region and a feeder share one table

def scaled_error(actual, forecast, train, m=7):
    v = train.to_numpy()
    scale = np.mean(np.abs(v[m:] - v[:-m]))
    err = np.mean(np.abs(actual - forecast))
    return err / scale
  • The numerator: mean absolute error on the test days.
  • The denominator: the error of "last week" inside the training data, one week ahead.
  • No unit. Below 1 beats that in-sample error.
  • Above 1 at 28 days is normal: the yardstick is one week ahead.

This is the mean absolute scaled error (MASE). Why not a percentage error: it explodes near zero, which feeders reach, and it punishes over-forecasts and under-forecasts unequally. Why the denominator comes from training data: it must not depend on the test window, or the scale itself would leak. From now on the number to watch is the distance to the floor, not the distance to 1.

Sources: Forecasting: Principles and Practice, 3rd edition, evaluating point forecast accuracy.
