Simple exponential smoothing

A weighted average that forgets the past at a rate you choose

def ses_level(values, alpha):
    level = values[0]
    for x in values[1:]:
        level = alpha * x + (1 - alpha) * level
    return level      # the forecast, for every h
  • The value k days back has the weight alpha * (1 - alpha) ** k.
  • alpha near 0: a long memory, a smooth level.
  • alpha = 1: the level is the last value. That is the naive forecast.
  • The forecast is flat: the same level for every day ahead.

Work the three-value example of the lab on the board: 10, 20, 30 with alpha 0.5 gives levels 10, 15, 22.5. With alpha 0.3 the last 7 days carry 92% of the weight; with 0.1, 52%. The whole ETS family grows from this one line: add a trend with its own weight, add a season with its own weight.

The ETS family

Level plus an optional trend plus an optional season

Error

Additive or multiplicative. It decides the shape of the intervals.

Trend

None, additive, or damped: a slope that fades with the horizon.

Season

None, additive or multiplicative, with one period: here 7 days.

The three letters of the model string are error, trend, season; "Z" lets the library choose by an information criterion. ARIMA

Difference until the level stops wandering, then model the memory

I · differencing

Work on changes, not levels. A seasonal difference subtracts the same weekday of last week.

AR · autoregression

Today depends on the last few days, each with a coefficient.

MA · moving average

Today depends on the last few forecast errors.

Written as. ARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q) with a period m: the first bracket for day-to-day memory, the second for week-to-week.

Where ETS describes a series by components, ARIMA describes it by its correlations. The two families overlap: simple exponential smoothing is ARIMA(0,1,1). For this course one model is enough: ARIMA(1,0,1)(0,1,1) with a period of 7, which is one seasonal difference with a short memory around it.

Sources: Forecasting: Principles and Practice, 3rd edition, chapter 9.

The autocorrelation at lag k is the correlation of the series with itself k days earlier, here on the three training years of the last fold. Reading orders from such a table is a craft; the practical rule is enough: difference until the correlations die out quickly, then keep the model small. The negative value at lag 7 after a seasonal difference is the usual sign that a seasonal moving-average term is needed.

Diagnostics

If the residuals still hold a pattern, the model has not finished

  • Residuals are the one-step errors inside the training data.
  • A finished model leaves noise: no autocorrelation.
  • The Ljung-Box test asks whether the first 14 autocorrelations together are larger than noise gives.
  • A small p-value: a pattern is left, and the model's intervals, which assume noise, cannot be trusted.
0.0001
p-value of the weekly ARIMA: not noise
acorr_ljungbox(residuals, lags=[14],
               model_df=3)["lb_pvalue"]

This is LO2's evidence: residual diagnostics beside the score. model_df is the number of fitted ARMA coefficients, here three. Two cautions for the room. A passed test is necessary and not sufficient: it looks one step ahead inside the training data, and the forecast is 28 steps ahead outside it. And with a thousand residuals the test finds small patterns: read the p-value with the plot.

Sources: statsmodels documentation; Forecasting: Principles and Practice, 3rd edition, residual diagnostics.

The rule

The calendar is known for the future. The temperature is not.

Known at the origin

Dates, weekdays, Ramadan, both Eids, national days. Use the true future values.

Not known at the origin

Any measured quantity: temperature, prices, the demand of another region. Only a forecast of it may be used.

In the labs. The future temperature is the normal for the date plus the anomaly of the origin day, fading by a fifth each day. That is what a desk without a weather service can know.

The helper U.temp_known implements the last line. It is a deliberately simple weather forecast: persistence that decays. A real desk would use a weather provider's forecast and must then backtest with the forecasts that were issued at each origin, not with the measured temperatures: archives of issued forecasts are the hard thing to get. Probabilistic forecasts

An 80% interval is a promise about frequency, and it can be checked

Quantile

The 0.9 quantile is a value demand stays under on 9 days in 10.

Interval

The 80% interval runs from the 0.1 to the 0.9 quantile.

Coverage

The share of actual values that fell inside. For an 80% interval it should be near 80%.

What goes wrong. Intervals are printed by every library and checked by almost nobody. Too narrow, and the planner is surprised twice as often as promised.

Connect to Day 1: Omar needs the 0.8 quantile, Hamad the 0.9. They act on the quantiles, so a quantile that is wrong costs money directly. Participants met intervals by simulation in DS-201; the new part is that a forecast's interval can be scored against what happened, fold by fold.

The score for a quantile

Pinball loss charges a quantile unequally for its two kinds of miss

def pinball(y, q, tau):
    return np.mean(np.where(
        y >= q,
        tau * (y - q),          # too low
        (1 - tau) * (q - y)))   # too high

pinball([10], [8], 0.9)    # 1.8
pinball([10], [12], 0.9)   # 0.2
  • A 0.9 quantile pays nine times as much for being too low as for being too high.
  • That is the cost structure of Day 1's planner: the quantile is the cost ratio.
  • At 0.5 it is half the absolute error. Lower is better.
  • Averaged over several quantiles it scores the whole forecast distribution.

Pinball loss is also called quantile loss. It rewards a quantile that is both calibrated and sharp: a very wide interval has perfect coverage and a poor pinball loss. That is why the course reports both. In the scorecard of Day 3 pinball loss is averaged over the five quantiles and divided by the same scale as the point error.

Sources: Forecasting: Principles and Practice, 3rd edition, evaluating distributional forecast accuracy.
