Leakage across time

Three doors through which the future reaches a forecast

1 · A feature

A column computed from the series itself that reaches past the origin: a centred average, a lag shorter than the horizon.

2 · A regressor

An outside variable used with values that were not known on the origin day: measured weather, revised figures.

3 · A choice

A model, setting or window picked because it scored best on the test folds. No column leaks; the decision does.

The first two were met on Days 2 and 3 one at a time. The third is the hardest to see because the code of the model is clean: the leak is in how the analyst used the results. The lab's notebook has one of each, in this order, and the score is recorded after every fix.

Source: Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich and Stitelman (2012), Leakage in Data Mining.

Lab 4.1 · step 2

A centred average contains the days being forecast

X["smooth"] = data.demand.rolling(7, center=True, min_periods=1).mean().reindex(dates)   # t-3 to t+3
X["smooth"] = data.demand.rolling(7).mean().shift(28).reindex(dates)       # ends 28 days before t
0.24 → 0.41
scaled error before and after the fix
The rule. A feature built from the target must end at least one horizon before the day it describes: here 28 days.

The centred mean of the target day includes the target itself and three days after it. A trailing mean is not enough either: for a forecast made 28 days ahead, yesterday's demand is not known. Shift by the horizon. The score nearly doubles and is still far too good.

temp = data.temp_c.reindex(dates)                                  # measured: fine up to the origin

scaled error before and after the fix
The rule. For every outside variable ask what its value was on the origin day, not what it turned out to be.

This is the oracle of Lab 2.3, met as a fault. U.temp_known gives the normal for the day plus the anomaly measured on the origin day, fading by a fifth each day: the temperature rule of this course. In a real desk the honest input is the weather forecast archived on the origin day; if no archive exists, start one today.

Combining forecasts

03
Combining forecasts

Methods that err in different directions cancel part of each other's error

  • The error of an average is never above the average of the errors.
  • The gain is larger the less the methods' errors move together.
  • Three models from one family, fed the same inputs, cancel little.

The three in Lab 4.3

Decompose then ETS: seasons only. ARIMA with annual Fourier terms: seasons and recent errors. Regression: temperature and calendar. Three views of one series.

The simple average has no settings. Nothing to tune means nothing to tune on the test window.

The first bullet is the triangle inequality applied day by day; it holds for the absolute error whatever the methods are. Forty years of forecasting competitions report the same finding: simple combinations are hard to beat, and estimated weights often do worse than equal ones because the weights carry their own estimation error.

Sources: Bates and Granger (1969), The Combination of Forecasts; Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos (2020), The M4 Competition.

Lab 4.4 · step 2

State what the bands did, in counts a planner can check

Not this

"The 80% prediction interval has an empirical coverage of 0.783 and the 95% interval of 0.945."

This

"In the 2025 backtest the dark band held on 78 of 100 days and the light band on 95 of 100."

Three rules. Counts, not probabilities. What was measured, not what was intended. The period it was measured on.

The band is called an 80% band and held on 78 days in 100: say 78. A planner who is told "8 in 10" and then counts will count 78 and trust the next note more. Natural frequencies are understood more reliably than percentages by readers of every background; the source is below.

Source: Gigerenzer and Edwards (2003), Simple tools for understanding risks: from innumeracy to insight, BMJ 327.

Lab 4.4 · step 3

A forecast note has six parts, and the planner reads the first three

1 · Decision

What is forecast, for which days, for which decision.

2 · Forecast

The range of the median, and the quantile the reader acts on.

3 · Uncertainty

What the bands mean and how often they held.

4 · Evidence

The backtest score against the floor, in one sentence.

5 · Limits

When not to use it, and when to ask for a rerun.

6 · Source

Data up to which day, the method in one line, who signs.

The order is the reader's, not the analyst's: method comes last and takes one line. The lab's note for January: the median runs between 2,257 and 2,554 MWh a day, and the upper edge of the dark band, the 0.9 quantile, reaches 3,025 MWh on the highest day. Omar buys on the 0.8 quantile, just inside that edge. The capstone note is this structure on two pages with the fan chart.

The part most notes leave out

Say when the forecast should not be used

  • Outside its scope: not for one feeder, not for hours within the day, not beyond 28 days.
  • When its assumptions break: a declared heat wave or dust storm; a holiday date that moves.
  • What it never forecasts: outages.

Why it protects the reader

Shahad Al-Tamimi asked for the peak day three years ahead. A 28-day forecast stretched to answer her would carry these bands into a question they were never measured on.

Each limit names a situation and an action: do not use, or ask for a rerun.

The limits come from the course's own findings: the temperature assumption (Lab 2.3), the calendar (Lab 2.3), the outage of March 2023 (Lab 1.2), the evidence at 28 days only (Lab 1.4). A limit that cannot be traced to something measured is decoration. The lab's check counts the situations named and looks for the two coverage numbers.
